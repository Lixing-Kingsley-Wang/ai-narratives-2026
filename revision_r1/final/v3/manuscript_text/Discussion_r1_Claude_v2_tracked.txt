Discussion
Principal Results
From 2021 to 2025, the evaluative stance of high-quartile medical-journal abstracts shifted in register more than in direction. Classifier-measured Advocacy declined consistently, Cautious Optimism remained the majority stance, and the critical share rose after 2022 and remained elevated. The rise appeared within both discourse and evaluative papers and remained after adjustment for genre, publication type, specialty, and region. Within critical papers, errors became a far more common main concern, patient safety stayed the most common one, and regulation appeared less often among the main concerns even as regulation-themed papers grew in number. Within the hallucination theme, factual error was more common than fabrication. Scrutiny also varied across specialties, first-author countries, and publication formats, and these differences remained after adjustment. These findings characterize published discourse, not AI capability, clinical deployment, or whether the expressed stances were correct.
The temporal pattern does not support a broad turn against AI. The clearest change was the retreat of unqualified promotion, while qualified favourable framing stayed dominant and explicit scrutiny rose modestly. The timing of that rise is notable. Critical share was flat from 2021 to 2022 and higher from 2023, the year in which generative-model papers became common among critical abstracts and error concerns roughly doubled. This timing suggests that the arrival of LLMs in clinical research brought a new object of evaluation, although our data cannot show that one caused the other. Because the rise appeared within both genres and after adjustment, changing corpus composition does not explain it alone. The level of critical stance depended on how the corpus was defined, because records missed by the prefilter were less often critical, while its trend was stable across definitions. The change was mainly one of tone, from broad promotion toward more qualified assessment.
The retreat of unqualified promotion appeared in all three measurements: the classifier's Advocacy label, a strong-endorsement category that did not require transformative language, and an estimate corrected against human coding. The second measure addresses the unsymmetrical design of the 5-point stance scale: Alarm captured plain reports of underperformance, whereas Advocacy required an emphasis on transformative potential, so a decline in Advocacy alone could have reflected a narrow category. Strong endorsement, defined without reference to any specific wording, declined as well. The corrected estimates also show that unqualified promotion could be more common than the Advocacy label suggests, because the classifier placed some records that the human coder read as Advocacy in Cautious Optimism. The corrected estimate still declined over time, so all three measures agreed on the direction of the change, while they differed in its level. The direction of the decline is therefore more secure than its size. The exploratory forward-validation pointed the same way at the level of individual claims, as few early Advocacy predictions were fully borne out. Because that analysis was Advocacy-only, single-rater, and retrieval-dependent, it can show where optimistic predictions overreached, but not whether early optimism in general was misplaced.
Alongside this retreat from unqualified promotion, the content of criticism became more concrete. Error concerns rose sharply, and patient safety remained the most common concern. Governance and regulation became less often one of the main concerns, although regulation-themed papers increased in number, so the falling share reflects more on the relative emphasis, not the absolute amount of researchers’ attention in the literature. The lexicon analysis also adds a nuance. Governance terms such as "oversight" and "accountability" became more frequent in 2025, mainly in the later part of abstracts, where conclusions and recommendations usually were written ([[MA:lexicon_decomposition]]). Governance may therefore have moved from being a paper's main concern to being part of its closing recommendations, such as a call for human oversight. A second possible reason, which we did not test, is that each critical record could carry at most three themes, so the rise of error concerns may have pushed regulation out of the main concerns.
That shift also requires care in how the hallucination theme is read. In natural-language-generation research, hallucination can include several forms of unsupported, unfaithful, or incorrect output [6]. Our theme therefore included both factual error and fabrication. The mechanism analysis found factual error more often than fabrication, even as generative-model papers became more common, and fabrication stayed at about one in five generative-model papers. This suggests that published criticism focused more on whether outputs were correct than on whether models invented content. The interpretation remains provisional. Human-model agreement for failure mode was moderate, and the classifier assigned fabrication more readily than the human coder, so the fabrication share should be interpreted as an upper bound.
Beyond what was criticized, scrutiny was uneven across specialties, first-author countries, and publication formats. Earlier regulatory studies found that AI-enabled medical-device approvals were concentrated in a small number of specialties, especially radiology [19,20]. Specialties with more mapped FDA-cleared devices had lower critical rates in our data, but this exploratory ecological association rests on 18 category-level units and was no longer statistically significant after categories without a mapped panel were removed. Several explanations are possible, and we offer them only as conjecture. Familiarity with fielded systems may temper alarm. Scrutiny may also concentrate where systems are least validated for routine use. Unmeasured specialty factors, such as task type, evidence conventions, or the maturity of AI applications in the field, may shape both criticism and clearance. The cross-sectional design cannot distinguish these accounts. The FDA count reflects US cleared-device availability only, not adoption, deployment, or clinical use.
First-author geography likewise indexes publication metadata, not national attitudes, research quality, or AI capability. The lower critical rate among East Asian first authors persisted after adjustment, within journals, and within specialty, so specialty mix and journal choice explain little of it. Publication norms, language, editorial practice, and other unmeasured factors may contribute. 
Publication-type differences also persisted after adjustment. Reviews were the least critical and most favourable format, and the gap with research articles widened when genre, specialty, and region were held constant. One legible reason is theme emphasis: reviews foregrounded governance and ethics, whereas primary research foregrounded concrete error. Within reviews, systematic reviews were more critical than non-systematic reviews, and the larger non-systematic group set the overall tone. Outside the medical-AI literature, clinical reviews have attracted more early page views than primary papers, and reviews have been overrepresented among highly downloaded articles in general medical and radiology journals [21-23]. These studies were not specific to our corpus and did not identify practising clinicians, but they suggest that publication format may affect the evaluative material readers encounter.
Comparison With Prior Work
Because no prior study has measured the evaluative stance of the medical-AI journal literature directly, these estimates cannot be benchmarked against earlier work. Bibliometric and scoping studies mapped the field's growth and topics [10,11], and the closest stance-adjacent work measured public sentiment toward health AI [12] or the general positivity of scientific abstracts [13,14]. Word-level studies have described rising positive language in scientific abstracts over time [13] [check that ref 13 reports this]. At the level of evaluative conclusions about AI, we found unqualified promotion receding while favourable framing stayed the majority, which suggests that word choice and evaluative commitment can move separately. LLM-assisted classification of scientific text can approach the agreement of human annotators when it is validated against human coding [15,16]; we extended that practice to every classification step, including corpus selection and concern themes.
Implications
Taken together, these results support more precise language in medical-AI reporting. Favourable conclusions can remain favourable while stating limits, uncertainty, and specific failure modes. Critical reporting should also distinguish factual error from fabrication instead of using hallucination as a single category. Calling both "hallucination" hides whether a model gave a wrong answer or invented one. Journals, peer reviewers, and reporting standards can treat that distinction as an editorial expectation rather than a stylistic preference: authors can be asked to state which failure they observed, so that critical findings remain comparable across papers and across model generations.
The distribution of scrutiny also matters for research communication. A reader may meet a different evaluative register across specialties and publication formats even when the topic is similar. Which editorial conventions, evidence standards, and article purposes drive that variation is worth closer study. Within this corpus, reviews were the least critical and most favourable format, while the readership studies noted above place reviews among the most downloaded articles in general medical and radiology journals. Readers, guideline developers, and educators who use syntheses as their main point of contact with this literature may therefore meet a more favourable evaluative register than the primary evaluations underneath it, and may wish to consult those evaluations directly when a synthesis informs a recommendation or a teaching point.
Limitations
These readings depend on how well the measurement worked. Every LLM classification step was validated against blinded human coding. Agreement was almost perfect for the prefilter and model type, substantial for stance and specialty, high for concern themes, and moderate for failure mode. Two steps bound what can be claimed. The prefilter decides which papers enter the corpus, and it missed about one in five AI-engaged records, including papers whose engagement with AI appears only later in the abstract. Missed records were less often critical, so the corpus overstates the level of critical stance, although the trends were similar when the missed records were added back. The failure-mode axis carries the accuracy-over-fabrication reading, and its fabrication estimates are treated as an upper bound.
The misclassification correction assumed that conversion rates between model and human labels were stable across publication years. All records were classified with the same prompt and settings in independent requests, and validation records were coded in random order, which makes stable rates plausible. A post hoc analysis that allowed the rates to differ between 2021–2023 and 2024–2025 yielded different, imprecise estimates, reflecting the small number of validation records in each period-specific stratum ([[MA:corrected_prevalence]]). Thus, although the decline in Advocacy and the rise in critical stance were consistent across alternative classification approaches, their misclassification-corrected magnitude and direction were sensitive to assumptions about temporal stability.
The analysis was limited to titles and abstracts, which compress findings and may not reflect qualifications in the full text. Validation depended on one human coder. The classifiers were hosted LLMs whose behaviour can drift, so the committed outputs, not re-inference, are the basis for exact reproduction. The theme classifier read only the first 400 characters of each abstract, although the coder, reading full abstracts, agreed closely with its labels.
The corpus was restricted to English-language, PubMed-indexed Q1/Q2 journals. Best-quartile matching admitted some journals through nonmedical categories, although restricting the corpus to medical categories gave similar results. Records from 2026 were incomplete and were reported only descriptively. First-author country was inferred from one affiliation and is an approximate proxy for publication geography. The FDA measure was US-centric, counted cleared devices, and assigned structural zeros through the panel mapping. Specialty, geographic, and format comparisons were cross-sectional and may be confounded by unmeasured factors, such as the maturity of AI applications in a field. Generative share was calculated among critical papers and was therefore endogenous to criticality. Multiple analyses were descriptive and hypothesis-generating; P values were not adjusted for multiplicity or used for confirmatory decisions. The forward-validation was exploratory, Advocacy-only, single-rater, and retrieval-dependent.
Reflexivity
We began this study expecting the critical stance to peak in 2023, after the first wave of LLM evaluations, and then to settle back toward optimism. Instead, the data showed a rise after 2022 that remained elevated, and the temporal question changed accordingly: not when criticism peaked, but how far unqualified promotion had receded. One author coded every validation sample and repeatedly adjudicated the boundary between Alarm and Caution, which is where our own reading of hedged conclusions was formed through research.
This study also used generative AI as its instrument while analysing how the medical literature evaluates generative AI, so the tool and the object belonged to the same class of system. Three moments made that relation visible. The classifier applied the fabrication label more readily than the human coder, so the very distinction we report, between factual error and fabrication, was shown unstable inside our own measurement. The unvalidated predictive-claim flag manufactured forward-looking predictions from descriptive text in 18 of 92 candidate records, which is why a human gate preceded every verdict. And when human-model agreement was allowed to differ between periods, the corrected trends moved, which showed how much our temporal claims rest on the assumption that the instrument reads every year alike. Following Christou [24], we treat reflexivity here as looking forward rather than turning back: analytic agency was distributed between the authors and the models, while accountability for the resulting classifications remains ours. We also acknowledge the constructivist risk he identifies, that delegating classification to a fast and fluent tool can reduce the interpretive friction through which a researcher notices ambiguity. Hand-coding the validation samples was our attempt to keep that friction; we cannot claim it survived intact across 14,058 records.
Conclusions
Within these limits, the picture is consistent. In high-quartile medical-journal abstracts from 2021 to 2025, classifier-measured unqualified promotion of AI declined while qualified optimism remained dominant, and criticism became more concrete, centering on errors and safety. Scrutiny varied across specialties, first-author geography, and publication formats. These differences were robust: they held after adjustment for publication year and the other record characteristics, and the geographic and publication-format differences also held within journals. Published concern focused more on factual accuracy than on fabrication. To our knowledge, this is the first validated measurement of evaluative stance in the medical-AI journal literature itself, with human validation of every LLM classification step. Two practical steps follow. Authors and journals can state limits alongside favourable conclusions, and critical reports can name whether a model gave a wrong answer or invented one. Readers who rely on reviews may meet a more favourable register than the primary evaluations beneath them.
